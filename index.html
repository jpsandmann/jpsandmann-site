In my work, I study the challenges to liberal democracy. Foregrounding how art and culture might deepen the appreciation for ambiguity and complexity that liberal democracies require, I seek to recover a once prominent humanistic perspective that may help defend liberal democracy against its critics.

My dissertation and book project, Between Art and Politics, develops this perspective by turning to an unusual suspect in political philosophy: the writer Thomas Mann (1875–1955). Not only do I reconstruct Mann’s account of how his own tradition of modern art and literature might help prepare citizens for life in a pluralistic, disagreement-torn society; I defend his view against critiques from postwar liberals, critical theorists, and postmodern thinkers on culture.

At Harvard, I’ve been an Edmond J. Safra Graduate Fellow in Ethics, a Graduate Affiliate of the Minda de Gunzburg Center for European Studies, and a Theodore H. Ashford Fellow. I’ve served as Head Teaching Fellow for Peter Gordon’s course on the German philosophical tradition from Nietzsche to Habermas, as well as for undergraduate and graduate-level classes in moral and political philosophy.

I hold a B.Sc. in Government and Economics and an M.Sc. in Philosophy of the Social Sciences, both from the London School of Economics, where I was funded by the Studienstiftung des deutschen Volkes. My research has been published in Philosophy & Social Criticism, Synthese, and the Nomos series Staatsverständnisse.
